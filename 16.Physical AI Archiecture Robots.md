It’s the weekend, but our work continues — and today we reached another important milestone in our new Physical AI architecture robot project.

This experiment addresses a seemingly small but actually fundamental engineering question:

How can we mount and drive a dexterous hand at the end of a nonlinear scissor robotic arm — without using a precision gearbox?
We cannot simply copy the human hand.

Once the physical architecture of the robot changes, the design logic of its end-effector must change as well. For our nonlinear scissor robot, there is no existing architecture we can simply copy. So we have to start from the structural and mechanical characteristics of the robot itself and develop our own solution:

Original design → Prototype → Experimental validation → Improvement → Iteration.

We have now taken an important step from structural concept toward a functional system.

This raises an interesting question:

Why can we eliminate the gearbox?

Conventional robots rely heavily on precision gearboxes because rotary joints often operate under high torque, high loads, and demanding mechanical conditions. Gearboxes allow motors to generate much higher output torque, but they also add cost, weight, mechanical complexity, transmission losses, and other engineering constraints.

Our approach starts from a different direction.

If we redesign the robot from its physical architecture — optimizing the load path and reducing the forces and torques that the actuators must overcome — some components that were traditionally considered indispensable may no longer be necessary.

Our latest experiment produced a particularly encouraging result:

The current experimental mechanism requires only about 5 watts of drive power to perform its intended function, with power still in reserve.

The 5 W figure itself is not the ultimate goal.

What matters more is the engineering principle behind it:

Instead of continuously adding larger motors and more expensive gearboxes to overcome the burden created by a heavy robotic structure, why not redesign the robot body so that it becomes inherently easier to move?

Lower drive power means lower energy consumption and reduced thermal load. For mobile and embodied robots, it can also translate into longer operating time from the same battery capacity.

AI is evolving rapidly.

But this leads us to another equally important question:

If the robot’s “brain” has entered the AI era, shouldn’t its “body” be redesigned for the AI era as well?

That is what we mean by:

Physical Architecture of AI.

Innovate. Validate. Iterate.

Today, we moved another step forward.
